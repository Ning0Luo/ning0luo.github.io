DES avalanche

Real DES, run twice on inputs that differ in exactly one bit. Flip a plaintext bit to watch diffusion carry it across the block; flip a key bit to watch confusion tie every ciphertext bit to the key through the S-boxes. Within five rounds about half of the 64 bits differ, and they stay that way.

Where each property lives in DES

DES is a Feistel network: each round computes f on the right half, XORs it into the left, and swaps. Inside f, E and P spread differences, the round key brings in the key, and the S-boxes make everything depend on it nonlinearly. The swap between rounds keeps both halves moving.

Diffusion

Expansion E

32 → 48 bits. Edge bits of each nibble are copied, so one bit feeds two neighbouring S-boxes.

Key mixing

⊕ round key

Ki is 48 of the 56 key bits, chosen by rotations and PC-2. Each key bit shows up in 12–15 of the 16 rounds.

Confusion

S-boxes S1–S8

6 → 4 bits, the only nonlinear step. A 1-bit input change always flips at least 2 output bits.

Diffusion

Permutation P

Sends the 4 outputs of each S-box to 4 different S-boxes in the next round.

Diffusion

Feistel swap

L ⊕ f becomes the new R, so the half that was just changed is the one mixed next round.

Things to try

  • Flip a plaintext bit and step through rounds 1–3. If the bit lands in L, round 1 only carries it across. From the next round on, count the active S-boxes and follow P as it fans their outputs out.
  • Switch to key bits and watch the round key K row. The flipped bit comes and goes from round to round, and whenever it is present it goes straight into an S-box.
  • Flip key bit 8, 16, …, or 64. These are parity bits and PC-1 drops them, so both runs are identical. DES has 56 key bits, not 64, which is why brute force costs 256.
  • Hit Random inputs a few times. The final count moves around 32 but rarely leaves 24–40, which is what a random 64-bit function would look like.
